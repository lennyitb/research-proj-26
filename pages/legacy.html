// 06 / the crackdown

FUCK THE POLICE

As phreaking grew from a scattered handful of loners into something closer to a movement, the community buckled under its own popularity. Old guard versus newcomers, explorers versus freeloaders, arguments about who belonged and who was going to get everyone caught. But it was too late. Phreaking had always been illegal; that had never changed. What changed was that the telephone company could no longer afford to ignore it. A handful of curious loners exploring the network's backwaters were a mere nuisance. But a movement — with zines and evangelists and college kids flooding trunk lines every night — was a serious threat.

Law enforcement had no idea what to do. There was no precedent, no playbook — just a group of people doing things with the telephone network that shouldn't have been possible, and mounting pressure from AT&T to make it stop. So they did what they have always done when confronted with something they don't understand: they treated curiosity as criminal intent and competence as threat.

The front page of the Wall Street Journal told the story plainly enough. In January 1976, the paper ran a piece cataloguing the accused: a singer who pleaded guilty in Los Angeles, an actor raided in his apartment and caught holding a blue box, a New York coin dealer who made calls to Israel, Germany, and Switzerland in a single twenty-four-hour period. AT&T's general security manager conceded that the users "probably consider themselves basically honest," then in the same breath called it a combination of greed and the attitude "that it's all right to rip off the phone company" (Jacobs, "Blue Boxes Spread From Phone Freaks To the Well-Heeled," Wall Street Journal).

Consider John Draper. He wiretapped the FBI from a payphone across the country — a kind of amazing feat. Bell engineers were certain this was impossible and for a few days it wasn't taken seriously until they encountered proof he did it a second time. He appeared before the same judge three times for the same crime. By any rational measure, he could have gone to prison for far longer than he did, and what he got was nine months. That is not a harsh sentence. And yet it breaks your heart, because Draper is genuinely not a criminal. He is a tinkerer, an evangelist — a man who discovered that a cereal whistle could command the telephone network and could not stop himself from telling everyone he met. Nine months in a cell for that (Fager, "Captain Crunch: The Unique Phreak"). The sentence was lenient, but I don't think he should have gone to jail at all.

Kevin Mitnick's story is worse. The most famous hacker in American history was held without bail after his 1995 arrest, in part because a federal prosecutor convinced a judge that Mitnick could "whistle into a phone and launch nuclear missiles." This was not possible. It was not close to possible. It was a fantasy constructed from fear and technical illiteracy, and a judge accepted it as grounds to deny bail. Mitnick spent years in detention, including time in solitary confinement. The treatment was so egregious it provoked the founding of the Electronic Frontier Foundation — an institutional civil liberties response from mainstream figures who recognized that the government was criminalizing activities it simply did not understand (Glance, "Kevin Mitnick's Story Shows Why We Shouldn't Use Jail to Make Examples of Hackers").

In the decades since, we have nominally figured this out. We have bug bounty programs, responsible disclosure frameworks, and an entire profession of penetration testing built on the premise that breaking into systems is not only legal but necessary. White-hat hackers are rarely prosecuted in the course of their work. The exploration that phreakers were imprisoned for is now, in its modern form, a career. You could be forgiven for thinking this means the problem is solved.

It is not. In 2011, Aaron Swartz downloaded academic journal articles from JSTOR through MIT's network. JSTOR itself declined to press charges. Federal prosecutor Carmen Ortiz pursued the case anyway, threatening up to 35 years in prison and a million dollars in fines for downloading PDFs of academic papers. Swartz killed himself in January 2013. He was twenty-six (Amsden, "The Brilliant Life and Tragic Death of Aaron Swartz"). The pattern that put Draper in a cell and Mitnick in solitary has not been retired. We have developed a code of ethics around hacking, but the people with the power to press charges have not always bothered to learn it. Lives were ruined, and the people responsible were never held accountable.
