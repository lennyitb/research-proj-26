// 03 / the meeting place

HIDING IN THE WALLS

Imagine you've just built your first blue box, or you've just learned to whistle a trunk line open, and it works. The network responds. You can route yourself anywhere on the planet, through tandems and toll switches that were never meant to hear your voice. You are, in the most literal sense, inside the telephone system — moving through its corridors, listening to its machinery hum. And it is an uneasy place to be. You are not supposed to be here. Every click on the line could be someone listening. The Bell System is enormous and gray and it does not know you're in its walls, but it could find out.

For a long time, that's all phreaking was: solitary exploration. A blind kid in Memphis whistling tones into a handset. A college student in Berkeley dialing all ten thousand numbers in a dead exchange to see what answers. A teenager in New Jersey building a box from a magazine schematic and aiming it at the nearest trunk. Each of them was alone inside the same system, mapping it by feel, and none of them knew about the others.

But the telephone network was vast, and it had seams — test lines, loop-arounds, open trunks, diagnostic circuits, quiet backwaters of the infrastructure that existed for maintenance or existed by accident. And one night, after weeks or months of exploring alone, you call one of these numbers and there are voices. In Los Angeles it might be the busy-signal verification line, where enough callers dialing in at once could hear each other underneath the tone. In New York it might be a disconnected-number recording — the gentle "the number you have reached is not in service" — playing on an open circuit while callers talked over it. Somewhere else it might be a conference bridge that another phreaker wired together through a chain of blue-boxed trunks. However you get there, the experience is the same: other people who went through the same thing you did. The same obsessive curiosity, the same late nights, the same knot in your stomach when the line clicks. They are your people, and you have found them in perhaps the most unlikely meeting place in the world — a gap in the phone company's infrastructure that wasn't designed to hold a conversation at all. The system had cracks in its walls, and people turned them into living rooms (Lapsley).

This is the moment where persistence pays off, and it's worth pausing on what it took to get here. The barrier to entry was not trivial. You needed technical knowledge, or perfect pitch, or the patience to scan thousands of numbers, or access to a blue box and the nerve to use it. You could not stumble into a phreaker conference call the way you could stumble into a chat room. The difficulty of arrival filtered the community with remarkable precision — the people who made it through were, almost by definition, the curious ones, the persistent ones, the ones who cared enough about understanding the system to risk real consequences for the privilege. Casual interest couldn't survive the climb.

And what they found on the other end was something no one else in the world had. Not metaphorically — no one. Before the consumer internet, before ARPANET reached beyond a handful of universities and defense labs, real-time, long-distance, many-to-many communication was technically possible — but as Lapsley writes, "about the only people who could afford conference calls were big businesses and the government." You called a special operator, had her connect each party, and paid AT&T's highest long-distance rate — "the so-called operator assisted rate, per person, per minute" (Lapsley, ch. 11). For everyone else, you picked up the phone, paid by the minute, and spoke to one person. That was the ceiling of human connection at a distance. Phreakers shattered it. They stitched together conference bridges, loop-arounds, and stolen trunk lines into something that had never existed before in the history of human communication: a living, breathing, multi-party network that spanned the continent, cost nothing, and ran all night. They were — and it is not an exaggeration to say this — the first people on Earth to experience anything resembling the internet.

Think about what that means. In the 1960s and '70s, while everyone else in the country was placing three-minute long-distance calls and watching the charges add up, a scattered handful of people were holding group conversations with dozens of strangers across the country for hours at a time. They had regulars, and in-jokes, and feuds, and romances. They had community. They had built, from inside the walls of a system that was never meant to carry any of this, something that the rest of the world wouldn't get for another twenty or thirty years. That is a superpower. Not a metaphorical one — a genuine, reality-bending asymmetry between what these people could do and what everyone around them understood to be possible. If you tried to explain it to an ordinary person in 1972, they would not have had a frame of reference. The concept of an online community didn't exist yet. Phreakers were living in the future, and they knew it, and the wonder of that — the sheer giddy strangeness of being the only people alive who had this — is the emotional core of every phreaker memoir and interview I've read.

This is what makes phreaking culture genuinely singular and not merely a precursor to what came later. It required a specific, unrepeatable collision of conditions: a telephone network whose control signals were audible and manipulable, a monopoly so total that it created a universal shared infrastructure to explore, a barrier to entry steep enough to filter for the devoted, and an era before the internet offered an easier path to the same kind of connection. Every one of those conditions has since vanished. The signalling tones phreakers relied on went "out of band" ie "out of their reach" (AT&T, Notes on the Network, sec. 6). The monopoly shattered. The internet arrived and made long-distance communication free and trivial for everyone. And that last part is the cruelest irony: the thing that phreakers had — instant, free, boundless connection with people who shared their obsessions — eventually became so universal that it stopped being remarkable at all.

"When everyone's super.... no one will be."

— Syndrome, The Incredibles

Today we have everything phreakers dreamed of and more, and are often bored by it. There will be communities that echo the spirit of phreaking, but the specific alchemy that produced it — a tribe of explorers who had to break into a system just to find each other, who were genuinely the first and only people on the planet to experience what they experienced — might not happen again in my lifetime.
