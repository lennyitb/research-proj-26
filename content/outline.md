Almost every story I read about phreaking has the same ring to it (no pun intended). More than anything, it's a story of wonder, the story of feeling like you've just gained a superpower, or maybe like you've conquered an infinite labyrinth. 

In a lot of ways, phreaking feels like the golden age of wonder, And the hackers of today are kind of just chasing that high. My foremost hope in this project is to share some of that wonder. 

## Home

Phone phreaking was the art of exploring — and exploiting — the telephone network. By whistling the right tones or building small electronic devices, phreakers could slip past the system's gates: making free calls, eavesdropping on internal lines, and routing themselves anywhere on the planet.

The first phreakers emerged in the late 1950s, but it took years for the practice to catch on. By the late '60s and into the '70s, it had grown into a full-blown underground movement — one that thrived for decades, only fading as the digital age arrived and the internet replaced the phone network as the frontier worth exploring.

But the real story isn't just the trick; it's what the trick made possible. Phreaking gave people superpowers of communication. While everyone else was paying by the minute and calling across town, phreakers were connecting across continents, for free, for hours. The community that grew out of that was unlike anything else in its era: farther reaching, more tightly knit, and wildly more vivid than it had any right to be.

## The Phreakers

While phreaking culture is more omnipresent than almost any other community of its day, this has the effect of making it feel scattered across time and space. Here are a few key figures, the common drive they share, the incredible challenges they each overcame, and the unique approaches they devised to find each other.

### Steve Wozniak

Wozniak's way in was the most methodical. He read Ron Rosenbaum's 1971 *Esquire* article, ["Secrets of the Little Blue Box,"](documents/rosenbaum1971.pdf) then went to the Stanford Linear Accelerator Center library to pull AT&T technical manuals and figure out the exact frequencies. He built a digital blue box — more precise than the analog ones others were using. And he explored the system with engineering patience: when he found a dead exchange in the phone book, he'd dial all 10,000 numbers in sequence to see what was hiding in there. His curiosity was systematic where Joybubbles' was instinctive. The famous line is that without the blue boxes, there would have been no Apple — the experience of building something small that could command something enormous shaped everything that came after.

### Joybubbles

Blind from birth, perfect pitch. Discovered as a kid that whistling certain tones into the phone made things happen — the system responded to his voice. His way in was purely physical and intuitive; he didn't need a schematic or a device, just his ears and his mouth. He could hear the network's control language and speak it back. He spent years mapping the system by sound, calling numbers and listening to what came back. Later legally changed his name to Joybubbles and declared himself a perpetual five-year-old (Marsh, ["The Legend of Joybubbles,"](https://mspmag.com/arts-and-culture/the-legend-of-joybubbles/) *Mpls.St.Paul Magazine*). His approach to phreaking was almost childlike in the literal sense — pure curiosity with no agenda beyond wanting to know what was on the other end of every sound.

joybubbles.png

### Cap'n Crunch

John Draper's way in was serendipity — he was told (likely by a blind phreaker) that the free toy whistle in a box of Cap'n Crunch cereal produced a tone at exactly 2600 Hz, the frequency the Bell System used to signal an open trunk line. He didn't discover it through technical study or natural ability; someone handed him the key and he tried the lock. From there he became an evangelist and a tinkerer, building blue boxes and spreading the knowledge. His role in the story is less about the method and more about the chain of transmission — how a piece of knowledge passed from person to person and what each one did with it.

Phreaking encapsulated both the intelectuals driven by curiosity, who invented the blue box, it also attracted something close to script kiddies, and there were multiple publications that aiming to stoke that crowd and supply them with instructions to exploit the system. The techniques of phreaking were also leveraged by a ton of people who just wanted free phone calls.

Throughout the history of phreaking; the time it was technically feasable or worthwhile, it perceptibly shifted from curiosity to focusing on plain wire fraud, as the masses became more and more aware. 

draper.jpg

### Abbie Hoffman

Hoffman's way in wasn't technical — it was ideological. Author of _Steal This Book_ and one of the era's most visible counterculture provocateurs, where the other phreakers explored the network out of curiosity, Hoffman saw free calls as something closer to a right, and the act of making them as political protest. He didn't care much about what the system sounded like on the inside; he cared about what it represented. His unique contribution was reframing phreaking from a feat of exploration into an act of defiance — taking techniques that had been passed around among hobbyists and recasting them as tools for the movement. In short, Hoffman sought to politicize phreaking with the hope that the masses' hatred of the Bell System could be leveraged to radicalize new members of the counterculture.

hoffman.jpg

### The Throughline

The people who made phreaking did it because of their curiosity, they were each driven to sneak into and explore the system. People all across the country were each called into this individual persuit. These people didn't go away, and they never stopped popping up anew either. **For a long time the focus and exclusivity of their club was protected by the barrier to entry of their meeting places.** However, eventually the word got out and their hobby was overwhelmingly taken over by the masses. It's hard to imagine this having happened without people like Hoffman.

## "The System"

phreak-psa-ad.jpg to the right of the body text - "A real Penna Bella ad warning college students against wire toll fraud"

In the era this project covers, you were not allowed to own your telephone. You rented it, from the only company you were allowed to rent it from. They were notorious for testing the electrical impedence of their telephone lines at night to detect the presense of a second unauthorized handset, and would send someone to your house to remove it (Clayton, ["Ma Bell Is After Extensions,"](https://www.thecrimson.com/article/1973/10/27/ma-bell-is-after-extensions-pif/) *The Harvard Crimson*).

Bell System Practices governed every task an employee could perform, down to the most mundane. The most famous example being [BSP 770-130-301](documents/770-130-301_i2_Aug52_Sweeping_tci_ocr.pdf), which laid out the correct procedure for sweeping the floors of System facilities. The document hesitates to use the term "broom" preferring "floor brush" instead.

Nearly everyone in America had the same relationship with the Bell System: you paid them, you resented them, and you couldn't do anything about it. They were less a corporation than a fact of life, something enormous and gray that stood between you and everyone you wanted to talk to.

## Hiding in the Walls

Imagine you've just built your first blue box, or you've just learned to whistle a trunk line open, and it works. The network responds. You can route yourself anywhere on the planet, through tandems and toll switches that were never meant to hear your voice. You are, in the most literal sense, inside the telephone system — moving through its corridors, listening to its machinery hum. And it is an uneasy place to be. You are not supposed to be here. Every click on the line could be someone listening. The Bell System is enormous and gray and it does not know you're in its walls, but it could find out.

For a long time, that's all phreaking was: solitary exploration. A blind kid in Memphis whistling tones into a handset. A college student in Berkeley dialing all ten thousand numbers in a dead exchange to see what answers. A teenager in New Jersey building a box from a magazine schematic and aiming it at the nearest trunk. Each of them was alone inside the same system, mapping it by feel, and none of them knew about the others.

But the telephone network was vast, and it had seams — test lines, loop-arounds, open trunks, diagnostic circuits, quiet backwaters of the infrastructure that existed for maintenance or existed by accident. And one night, after weeks or months of exploring alone, you call one of these numbers and there are voices. In Los Angeles it might be the busy-signal verification line, where enough callers dialing in at once could hear each other underneath the tone. In New York it might be a disconnected-number recording — the gentle "the number you have reached is not in service" — playing on an open circuit while callers talked over it. Somewhere else it might be a conference bridge that another phreaker wired together through a chain of blue-boxed trunks. However you get there, the experience is the same: other people who went through the same thing you did. The same obsessive curiosity, the same late nights, the same knot in your stomach when the line clicks. They are your people, and you have found them in perhaps the most unlikely meeting place in the world — a gap in the phone company's infrastructure that wasn't designed to hold a conversation at all. The system had cracks in its walls, and people turned them into living rooms (Lapsley).

This is the moment where persistence pays off, and it's worth pausing on what it took to get here. The barrier to entry was not trivial. You needed technical knowledge, or perfect pitch, or the patience to scan thousands of numbers, or access to a blue box and the nerve to use it. You could not stumble into a phreaker conference call the way you could stumble into a chat room. The difficulty of arrival filtered the community with remarkable precision — the people who made it through were, almost by definition, the curious ones, the persistent ones, the ones who cared enough about understanding the system to risk real consequences for the privilege. Casual interest couldn't survive the climb.

And what they found on the other end was something no one else in the world had. Not metaphorically — no one. Before the consumer internet, before ARPANET reached beyond a handful of universities and defense labs, real-time, long-distance, many-to-many communication was technically possible — but as Lapsley writes, "about the only people who could afford conference calls were big businesses and the government." You called a special operator, had her connect each party, and paid AT&T's highest long-distance rate — "the so-called operator assisted rate, per person, per minute" (Lapsley, ch. 11). For everyone else, you picked up the phone, paid by the minute, and spoke to one person. That was the ceiling of human connection at a distance. Phreakers shattered it. They stitched together conference bridges, loop-arounds, and stolen trunk lines into something that had never existed before in the history of human communication: a living, breathing, multi-party network that spanned the continent, cost nothing, and ran all night. They were — and it is not an exaggeration to say this — the first people on Earth to experience anything resembling the internet.

Think about what that means. In the 1960s and '70s, while everyone else in the country was placing three-minute long-distance calls and watching the charges add up, a scattered handful of people were holding group conversations with dozens of strangers across the country for hours at a time. They had regulars, and in-jokes, and feuds, and romances. They had community. They had built, from inside the walls of a system that was never meant to carry any of this, something that the rest of the world wouldn't get for another twenty or thirty years. That is a superpower. Not a metaphorical one — a genuine, reality-bending asymmetry between what these people could do and what everyone around them understood to be possible. If you tried to explain it to an ordinary person in 1972, they would not have had a frame of reference. The concept of an online community didn't exist yet. Phreakers were living in the future, and they knew it, and the wonder of that — the sheer giddy strangeness of being the only people alive who had this — is the emotional core of every phreaker memoir and interview I've read.

This is what makes phreaking culture genuinely singular and not merely a precursor to what came later. It required a specific, unrepeatable collision of conditions: a telephone network whose control signals were audible and manipulable, a monopoly so total that it created a universal shared infrastructure to explore, a barrier to entry steep enough to filter for the devoted, and an era before the internet offered an easier path to the same kind of connection. Every one of those conditions has since vanished. The signals went out of band (AT&T, [*Notes on the Network*](documents/notn1980.pdf), sec. 6). The monopoly shattered. The internet arrived and made long-distance communication free and trivial for everyone. And that last part is the cruelest irony: the thing that phreakers had — instant, free, boundless connection with people who shared their obsessions — eventually became so universal that it stopped being remarkable at all. To quote Syndrome from _The Incredibles_: "When everyone's super.... no one will be". Today we have everything phreakers dreamed of and more, and are often bored by it. There will be communities that echo the spirit of phreaking, but the specific alchemy that produced it — a tribe of explorers who had to break into a system just to find each other, who were genuinely the first and only people on the planet to experience what they experienced — might not happen again in my lifetime.

## A Culture of Pranking

The telephone network was not made only of switches and trunks. It was also made of people. Operators sat at the seams of the system, routing calls, providing information, and enforcing the rules of the Bell System. Phreakers had been manipulating them from the very beginning. Social engineering — talking an operator into connecting you somewhere you weren't supposed to go, or into reading back information she wasn't supposed to share — was a core phreaking skill, as fundamental as the blue box itself. But it was utilitarian, and it was turned inward: you manipulated the operator because the operator was part of the network, a human switching node standing between you and the next trunk. You were still exploring the system. The operator just happened to be one of its components.

Pranking was not a change in posture. It was the same impulse — what can I make this system do? — simply turned around. Instead of manipulating inward, toward the infrastructure, you manipulated outward, toward the people on the other end of the line. The curiosity was identical. The direction was different.

And the results were spectacular. Wozniak, who never missed a chance to test what his blue box could reach, once called the Vatican and, posing as Henry Kissinger, asked to speak with the Pope. He got as far as the bishop who would have woken the Pope up before he cracked and hung up (Wozniak, ["Playful Pranks from Apple's Founder,"](https://www.npr.org/2009/01/03/98977379/playful-pranks-from-apples-founder) NPR). The gag wasn't theft; it wasn't even really defiance. It was the purest expression of the question that had driven every phreaker into the network in the first place: what happens if I try this?

Others tested the same question with escalating audacity. In 1974, two phreakers exploited a switching bug to intercept incoming calls to Santa Barbara, telling each bewildered caller that a nuclear explosion had knocked out all the telephone lines — an improvised hoax that sparked panic from coast to coast ([Lapsley](https://www.salon.com/2013/02/16/from_phreaks_to_apple_steve_jobs_and_steve_wozniaks_eureka_moment/)). They roused President Nixon to the phone to inform him of a toilet paper shortage in LA (["The Early Days of Phreaking,"](http://www.textfiles.com/phreak/PHREAKING/phreakh.ist) textfiles.com). They seized trunk lines and rerouted calls through a dozen countries — just to see if the signal would make it back. Every prank was an experiment with an audience: you pushed the system until something happened, and the something that happened was a person on the other end of the line reacting to an impossible situation.

These stories traveled back to the conference bridges and later to the BBSes, gaining detail with every retelling. They were performances for the community. You pulled something audacious, and then you brought the story home. The more impossible the prank, the more it proved your command of the system and your nerve in using it. Bragging rights were real currency — a great prank story could make your reputation on a conference call the way a great exploit would later make it on IRC.

But underneath the ego and the one-upmanship, the pranks were still made of wonder. You could make the Pope's bishop pick up the phone at three in the morning. You could make a stranger in Santa Barbara believe, for one terrible moment, that a nuclear war had started. You could make the telephone system — this vast, gray, implacable thing that everyone else simply obeyed — dance. That is funny, and it is also beautiful, and in a way it's the apex of mastery over the skill of phreaking. The system was yours, and you could make it do anything.

## Mainstream Invasion

The barrier to entry that had protected phreaking's inner world was starting to crack. Abbie Hoffman saw the telephone system the way he saw every American institution: as something that deserved to be stolen from on principle. His newsletter YIPL (Youth International Party Line, later renamed TAP) — significant enough to earn its own [FBI file](documents/yipl-fbi.pdf) — published phreaking techniques alongside pipe-bomb recipes and shoplifting guides, all under the same ideological umbrella — the system is corrupt, so take what you can. He didn't care about the network's interior life, its tones and tandems and quiet diagnostic lines. He cared about the political charge of the act. Every new reader who learned to make a free call was, in Hoffman's frame, a small soldier in a larger war. Meanwhile John Draper — Cap'n Crunch — was minting phreakers with the zeal of a missionary, teaching anyone who'd listen how to build a blue box, even as the FBI was actively building a case against him. He'd eventually go to prison for it, twice, and keep evangelizing after each release. Between them, Hoffman and Draper ensured that phreaking knowledge bled outward far faster than the community could absorb the newcomers.

The effect was immediate and predictable. When a conference line's number appeared in a zine or got passed around a college dorm, it would fill with voices within days — people who hadn't built anything, hadn't mapped anything, hadn't spent months alone inside the network earning their way to that exact frequency. They were there for the free calls, or for the thrill of doing something vaguely illegal, or because someone handed them a number and said try this. And then, just as quickly, the line would go dead. The phone company monitored its infrastructure; a trunk that suddenly carried dozens of unauthorized calls per night was not hard to find. The pattern became grimly routine: a piece of knowledge escapes, the curious masses flood in, the phone company notices, the resource disappears. Every conference bridge that died this way was a living room that someone else's carelessness had burned down (Lapsley, chs. 11, 14).

The old guard watched this happen and drew the obvious conclusion: the barrier to entry hadn't been incidental. It was structural. It was what kept the community small enough to survive, serious enough to be worth belonging to, and quiet enough to avoid attracting the attention that would destroy it. The people who made it through — the ones who had perfect pitch, or built their own boxes, or scanned ten thousand numbers — shared something beyond technical ability. They shared a disposition. The newcomers, by and large, did not. They wanted the output of phreaking without its process, the free call without the exploration, the conference bridge without having earned the right to be on it. As one old-guard phreaker put it, the newcomers "receive information and abuse its uses until the information is no longer valid" (Big Brother, ["A Lesson in Phreaking and Hacking Morality,"](http://www.textfiles.com/phreak/PHREAKING/morality.txt) textfiles.com).

This is where self-regulation entered the picture, and it carried an uncomfortable irony. The Bell System was a regulated monopoly — it policed its own behavior specifically to prevent the government from doing it more harshly. Now the phreaker community found itself in a mirror image of that same dynamic: policing its own members to prevent the phone company from cracking down more harshly on everyone. Don't publish that number. Don't bring strangers onto the bridge. Don't use a blue box for something as pedestrian as a free call to your girlfriend — you'll get us all caught. The community that had defined itself by breaking rules was now writing its own — "BOX THOU NOT OVER THINE HOME TELEPHONE WIRES," "MAKEST SURE THINE FRIENDS ARE INSTANT AMNESIACS" (TAP #86, ["The Phone Phreak's Ten Commandments,"](http://www.textfiles.com/phreak/PHREAKING/commandm.ent) textfiles.com) — and enforcing them with the same logic their enemy used: if we don't govern ourselves, someone worse will govern us.

It stratified them. There were now tiers — the explorers and builders at the top, the social engineers and pranksters in the middle, and at the bottom, a growing mass of people the old guard didn't recognize as phreakers at all. The word hadn't changed, but what it pointed to had split. To the originals, phreaking was a practice of understanding. To the newcomers, it was a trick. The community that had once been unified by the sheer difficulty of showing up was now divided by the question of what counted as legitimate membership — and there was no authority to settle it, because the whole enterprise existed outside of any structure that could grant authority. They were arguing about who belonged in a room that wasn't supposed to exist. One phreaker who came in late enough to feel the loss wrote, "God, how I wish I could of been there in the heyday of Lex Luthor, Captain Crunch, and the like" — and issued a plea to convert the "k0dez kidz" into "sincere members" by actually teaching them (Kodiak, ["An Appeal to All,"](http://www.textfiles.com/phreak/PHREAKING/appeal1) textfiles.com).

## Fuck the Police

As phreaking grew from a scattered handful of loners into something closer to a movement, the community buckled under its own popularity. Old guard versus newcomers, explorers versus freeloaders, arguments about who belonged and who was going to get everyone caught. But it was too late. Phreaking had always been illegal; that had never changed. What changed was that the telephone company could no longer afford to ignore it. A handful of curious loners exploring the network's backwaters were a mere nuisance. But a movement — with zines and evangelists and college kids flooding trunk lines every night — was a serious threat.

Law enforcement had no idea what to do. There was no precedent, no playbook — just a group of people doing things with the telephone network that shouldn't have been possible, and mounting pressure from AT&T to make it stop. So they did what they have always done when confronted with something they don't understand: they treated curiosity as criminal intent and competence as threat.

Consider John Draper. He wiretapped the FBI from a payphone across the country; a kind of amazing feat. Bell engineers were certain this was impossible and for a few days it wasn't taken seriously until they encountered proof he did it a second time. He appeared before the same judge three times for the same crime. By any rational measure, he could have gone to prison for far longer than he did, and what he got was nine months. That is not a harsh sentence. And yet it breaks your heart, because Draper is genuinely not a criminal. He is a tinkerer, an evangelist — a man who discovered that a cereal whistle could command the telephone network and could not stop himself from telling everyone he met. Nine months in a cell for that. The sentence was lenient, but I don't think he should have gone to jail at all (Fager, ["Captain Crunch: The Unique Phreak"](https://thephoenix.com/boston/news/22802-captain-crunch-the-unique-phreak/)).

Kevin Mitnick's story is worse. The most famous hacker in American history was held without bail after his 1995 arrest, in part because a federal prosecutor convinced a judge that Mitnick could "whistle into a phone and launch nuclear missiles." This was not possible. It was not close to possible. It was a fantasy constructed from fear and technical illiteracy, and a judge accepted it as grounds to deny bail. Mitnick spent years in detention, including time in solitary confinement (Glance, ["Kevin Mitnick's Story Shows Why We Shouldn't Use Jail to Make Examples of Hackers"](https://theconversation.com/kevin-mitnicks-story-shows-why-we-shouldnt-use-jail-to-make-examples-of-hackers-41038)). The treatment was so egregious it provoked the founding of the Electronic Frontier Foundation — an institutional civil liberties response from mainstream figures who recognized that the government was criminalizing activities it simply did not understand.

In the decades since, we have nominally figured this out. We have bug bounty programs, responsible disclosure frameworks, and an entire profession of penetration testing built on the premise that breaking into systems is not only legal but necessary. White-hat hackers are rarely prosecuted in the course of their work. The exploration that phreakers were imprisoned for is now, in its modern form, a career. You could be forgiven for thinking this means the problem is solved.

It is not. In 2011, Aaron Swartz downloaded academic journal articles from JSTOR through MIT's network. JSTOR itself declined to press charges. Federal prosecutor Carmen Ortiz pursued the case anyway, threatening up to 35 years in prison and a million dollars in fines for downloading PDFs of academic papers. Swartz killed himself in January 2013. He was twenty-six (Amsden, ["The Brilliant Life and Tragic Death of Aaron Swartz"](https://www.rollingstone.com/culture/culture-news/the-brilliant-life-and-tragic-death-of-aaron-swartz-177191/)). The pattern that put Draper in a cell and Mitnick in solitary has not been retired. We have developed a code of ethics around hacking, but the people with the power to press charges have not always bothered to learn it. Lives were ruined, and the people responsible were never held accountable.
